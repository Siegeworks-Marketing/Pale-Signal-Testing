GBA Hardware Reference
Screen: 240×160px • ~60.75fps • 3.5" LCD
Color: 15-bit BGR5 = 32,768 colors. No true black — min visible ≈ #080808
Palettes: 16×16 colors (256 total). Index 0 per palette = transparent
Sprites: 128 OAM. Sizes: 8×8 to 64×64. 4bpp = 16 colors per sprite
Backgrounds: 4 BG layers (BG0–BG3). Mode 1 = 2 tile BGs + 1 affine
Golden Sun: Mode 0 for towns/dungeons. Mode 1 affine BG3 for world map rotation
Pokémon RSE/FRLG: Mode 0. Pokémon sprites: 64×64, 16 colors. Trainer: 80×80

Baked Art Knowledge — World Council
▸ Hue-Shift Shading — The Core Secret
Why not just darken? Pure dark = muddy. Hue-shifting creates the illusion of light because real light scatters wavelengths differently.
Shadow rule (GS/Pokémon): Shift hue −15° to −30° toward blue-purple (220–260°). Reduce saturation 20–30%. Drop lightness 30–45%.
Highlight rule: Shift hue +10° to +20° toward amber-yellow (40–60°). Slight saturation boost. Raise lightness 25–40%.
GOLDEN SUN example: Isaac's green tunic — shadow is a desaturated blue-green, highlight is a warm yellow-green. The midtone is the only "true" green.
POKÉMON example: Charizard's orange body — shadow shifts toward dark red-brown, highlight toward pale yellow. Outline is darkened orange, not black.
▸ Golden Sun — Camelot's Full Technique
1px black outline on all characters. Reads at 8×8, 16×16, 32×32. Never anti-aliased. The outline IS the silhouette.
3-tone sprites: shadow / midtone / highlight + black outline = 4 total per material. Never more. The constraint produces clarity.
Inner outline trick: Add 1px of a mid-dark tone inside the black outline to separate body from limbs without adding a new color.
Palette animation: Djinn summons cycle palette entries per frame. Same pixel data, rotating colors = spell effects at zero VRAM cost.
Dithered BG gradients: GS uses Bayer 4×4 for sky and water tiles. Every pixel is one of two colors — dither creates gradient illusion.
Area color identity: Vale=warm green+brown, Imil=white+icy blue, Xian=warm red-orange, Mercury Lighthouse=cold blue-white, Sol Sanctum=deep purple+gold.
Portrait area: GS dialogue portraits are ~56×64px, using 15 full colors. They break the 3-tone sprite rule — much more painterly.
▸ Retro RPG Style — Sugimori Color Language (GBA Era)
Type = hue family: Fire=reds/oranges, Water=blues/cyans, Grass=greens, Electric=yellows, Psychic=pinks/purples, Dragon=blue-purples, Dark=dark browns.
Dark outline, not black: FRLG/RSE outlines are the darkest shade of the body color, not pure black. Pure black outlines look harsh at 64×64.
High saturation primary: The "body color" (midtone) is aggressively saturated — S=80-95%. GBA LCD can barely show it, which reads as vibrant.
Limited shadow tones: Pokémon sprites typically use 3–5 colors including outline. Shadow is often the body color shifted −30–40° cooler.
Highlight is near-white: Pokémon highlights are often 90–95% lightness with very low saturation — simulates glossy/shiny surface (many Pokémon are depicted as healthy/moist).
Two-color eyes: All Pokémon eyes use exactly 2 colors: dark pupil + white highlight pixel. 1 pixel = full emotional read.
Shiny variant: Shiny Pokémon reuse same sprite, different palette only. Always design sprites with palette-swap potential.
▸ Color Theory — Itten, Albers, Gurney
Value first (Itten): Print grayscale. If unreadable, contrast is broken. Value carries form. Hue carries emotion. Saturation carries energy.
Simultaneous contrast (Albers): A gray swatch looks warmer on blue, cooler on orange. Two identical colors look different depending on surroundings. Use this — don't fight it.
Temperature (Gurney): Warm light = cool shadows. Cool light (overcast) = warm shadows. Never both warm. GS outdoor scenes: warm gold light + cool blue-purple shadows.
Saturation falloff (Leonardo): Distant objects desaturate toward atmosphere color. GS distant mountains = desaturated blue-grey. Only foreground is fully saturated.
Complementary vibration: Complementary colors at full saturation vibrate at borders (simultaneous contrast × 2). Isaac's blue/orange is exactly this. Use sparingly — it's loud.
▸ Master Artists → Direct Pixel Applications
Rembrandt (1606–1669): Single light source. Everything else is cast into shadow. One bright pixel surrounded by shadow = Rembrandt spot. Use on shield emblems, gems, eyes.
Hokusai (1760–1849): Great Wave uses 3 values. Zero unnecessary information. Ukiyo-e woodblock = flat color areas with bold outline. This IS sprite design.
Monet (1840–1926): Pure adjacent hues, never blended. Pixel art IS Monet's technique — adjacent pure pixels create optical blending. His serial studies: light = color temperature shift.
Cézanne (1839–1906): All form = sphere, cone, cylinder. GBA sprites must commit to this — a character's head IS a sphere. Shade it as one.
Turner (1775–1851): Warm glowing center, cool fading edges. GS battle scenes put the warm action center against cool dark edges. Atmospheric.
▸ Advanced Pixel Art Principles
Pillow shading (AVOID): Shading that follows the outline — looks inflated, flat. Always shade toward a consistent light source, not the edge.
Banding (AVOID): Parallel shading bands that don't describe form. Each shade change should show a plane change or surface normal change.
Selective AA: Place 1 intermediate-value pixel on major diagonal steps of curves. GS does this on character hair and armor edges. Never on straight lines.
Sub-pixel rendering: A 1px element with AA pixels on sides reads as physically larger than it is. GS uses this on thin sword blades.
Economy principle: Remove pixels until it breaks. Add exactly one back. Every pixel must earn its place. GS sprites feel rich because no pixel is wasted.
Color count discipline: Any single material (skin, cloth, metal) should use max 4 colors. Silhouette can share colors with interior. Never introduce a new color when a shifted hue works.
